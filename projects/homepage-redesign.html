Case Study

Homepage Redesign + Global Block System

Transforming a static homepage into a dynamic mission hub — and a stronger engine for donations, engagement, and connection.

My Role

Team

The Problem

"The homepage of a nationwide nonprofit is often the first — and sometimes only — impression a visitor gets. Ours wasn't working hard enough."

  • Donation entry point was a single header button with low visibility & conversion rates.
  • No geographic personalization — users had to hunt for state-specific programs & resources.
  • Events and programs were scattered throughout the site with no clear wayfinding.
  • Mission, urgency, nor community was communicated through the homepage.

Goals

  • Increase donor conversion by surfacing a prominent, embedded donation form.
  • Reduce friction for users seeking immediate local resources & programming.
  • Communicate organizational identity — global research, nationwide advocacy, local care — at a glance.
  • Drive event participation and volunteerism through clearer pathways.
  • Deepen emotional connection with the mission through story.

Discovery & Research

Before drafting the solution, I needed to understand how visitors were actually using the homepage and where we were losing them. I reviewed traffic analytics, heatmaps, and conducted stakeholder interviews with internal teams including marketing, programs, and development (fundraising).

Findings

  • High bounce rates and shallow scroll depth on the homepage.
  • 13% clickthrough rate on the donation button in the header — well below industry standards.
  • Users from state-specific email campaigns were landing on the homepage with no clear path to local resources.
  • Flagship fundraising events like Walk to Defeat ALS were underrepresented on the homepage despite being top traffic drivers.

The Solution

The redesigned homepage is structured as a progressive narrative — orienting visitors, connecting them to local relevance, showcasing the breadth of the mission, and building toward emotional commitment.

  1. Hero + Embedded Donation Form

    Moved the donation experience from a single header button to a full hero unit with an embedded form offering one-time and monthly giving. This reduced the steps to donate and surfaced the action at zero scroll depth.

  2. Geolocation Block

    Detects user location and directs users to state-specific pages with program, resource, and event information. This solves the core wayfinding problem for users arriving from state-level campaigns or searching for local help.

  3. Nationwide Programs & Services Quick Links

    Provides a scannable navigation block for users who know what they're looking for. This reduces reliance on the main nav and flattens the path to key support destinations.

  4. Mission Priorities Block

    Communicates the three pillars — global research, nationwide advocacy, local care — in a visual, digestible format. Answers the first question any new visitor has: what does this organization actually do?

  5. Get Involved Carousel

    Surfaces the full range of engagement pathways: Walk to Defeat ALS, marathon and cycling events, community events, the annual conference, corporate partnerships, volunteering, and advocacy. Designed to serve both first-time visitors and returning supporters looking for new ways to connect.

  6. Organizational Story Block

    Provides a deeper narrative about the organization's founding, mission, and commitment to research and a cure. Builds trust and emotional resonance — especially important for major gift prospects and first-time visitors evaluating the organization.

  7. Monthly Donor CTA

    Closes the page with a high-impact ask: a personal story, quote, and image from someone living with ALS, paired with a direct invitation to join the monthly giving program. Uses emotional narrative to motivate the highest-value conversion on the page.

Key Decisions

Embedded form vs. traditional donate button?

The embedded form places the donation experience directly on the user's page — no redirect, no extra click.

  • Fewer steps = higher conversion. Every additional page load loses donors.
  • Donors never leave the site, so trust stays high and branding stays consistent.
  • Amounts, impact statements, and social proof can sit right alongside the form.

Why geolocation?

Geolocation transforms a one-size-fits-all experience into one that feels personally relevant to each user, which drives both higher donor conversion and higher program uptake from our constituents.

Prioritizing the block order

Feel → Find your place → Trust → Be inspired → Act.

Cuts and push-back

A newsletter sign-up embed was cut because it required a reliable two-way sync with Salesforce that couldn't be guaranteed. A broken or duplicating form creates data hygiene problems that cost more to fix than the form is worth. We moved forward with capturing emails through the donate flow and registration for programs and services instead, and will revisit the standalone embed once the Salesforce integration is stable.

Aligning stakeholders

  1. Show who's visiting and what they're doing, via user personas.
  2. Determine the most important actions — inspiring donation and connecting resources.
  3. Separate important from first.
  4. Propose an order with reasoning.
  5. Address concerns & establish a review period.

Reflection

This homepage redesign reinforced something I carry into every project: structure is strategy. The order of blocks isn't decoration — it's an argument about what matters and when. The hardest part of this project wasn't the design; it was aligning stakeholders with competing priorities across fundraising, programs, and marketing — each of whom had legitimate claims on homepage real estate. Building a shared framework around user needs, and tying every block to a specific audience job, gave me a neutral language for those conversations.

If I were to do this again, I'd invest more heavily in pre-launch usability testing with actual donors and community members, rather than relying primarily on internal stakeholder input and analytics. The geolocation block in particular would have benefited from more direct user feedback earlier in the process.
