Case Study

Navigation Overhaul

Five entry points into a sprawling national nonprofit site. Users hit a wall, backtracked, and gave up. I redesigned the navigation from the ground up — restructuring the IA and implementing a dropdown system that opened up 72 previously buried pages and let users choose their own path.

Challenge

A navigation system should work like a map — it should show you where you are, where you can go, and make the path obvious. Ours was doing none of that. Five buttons sat at the top of every page, each leading to a section landing page. From there, users relied entirely on side navigations buried within each section to go deeper. If you didn't already know where something lived, you weren't going to find it.

For a nationwide nonprofit with programs spanning local patient care, global research, nationwide advocacy, fundraising events, and volunteer opportunities, this was a serious problem. Entire categories of content — resources, research initiatives, ways to get involved — were functionally invisible to anyone who didn't already know to look for them.

The core issues:

  • 5 flat top-level buttons gave users no preview of what lived beneath them.
  • Deep content was only accessible via section-specific side navs, requiring users to first land in the right section before they could navigate within it.
  • Users couldn't move laterally across the site — jumping from, say, a research page to an advocacy resource required returning to a section landing page first.
  • Mobile navigation mirrored this flat structure, compounding the problem on smaller screens.
  • High-value pages were effectively hidden, depressing traffic and engagement.

Goals

  • Surface deep and underutilized content directly from the global navigation.
  • Enable lateral movement across the site without requiring detours through landing pages.
  • Create a consistent navigation experience across desktop and mobile.
  • Maintain and complement the existing side navigation system rather than replacing it.
  • Reduce the number of steps to reach key destinations.

Discovery

We started by auditing the existing site structure and cross-referencing it with analytics to identify which pages were being reached — and which weren't. The gap was significant. A large portion of our content was receiving minimal organic traffic, not because users weren't interested, but because they had no clear path to it. We supplemented the analytics review with usability findings and stakeholder interviews across programs, marketing, and development.

Findings

  • Analytics showed users were frequently exiting from section landing pages rather than navigating deeper — they arrived, didn't see an obvious next step, and left.
  • Pages per session was low, suggesting users weren't discovering related content.
  • Nav click data showed the 5 top-level buttons were being used unevenly — clicks were heavily concentrated on one or two sections, while others were rarely touched.
  • Usability findings revealed users were unaware that side navs existed until they were pointed out.
  • Stakeholders across programs and marketing independently flagged the same problem: content they considered high-priority was going unseen.

Solution

The redesigned navigation system introduces a responsive dropdown on desktop and a restructured mobile menu — both built to work in tandem with the existing side navigation rather than replace it. The global nav now gives every user, on every page, a full map of the site's most important destinations. The side nav retains its role as the guide for depth within a section.

Reflection

Navigation projects have a way of surfacing every unresolved question an organization has about itself — who are we for, what do we lead with, what do we hide? The IA work at the center of this project was as much organizational as it was design. Getting alignment required building a shared framework that could outlast any individual stakeholder's preference: we kept returning to user tasks and click data as the neutral arbiter when conversations got political.

The tandem system — dropdown for movement, side nav for depth — was the decision I'm most satisfied with in retrospect. It would have been easier to consolidate everything into one nav pattern, but it would have served users worse. The two systems do genuinely different jobs, and honoring that distinction made both of them better.

If I were to approach this again, I'd run formal tree testing earlier in the process, before any visual design work began. We relied heavily on analytics and stakeholder input to validate the IA, and while both were valuable, direct user testing of the hierarchy would have given us faster, cleaner signal on the decisions that mattered most.
